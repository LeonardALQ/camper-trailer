# 03: Bike restraint and garage fit-out

Note added 20 Nov 2026, for the LandCruiser 79: the trailer no longer has a garage. The bikes ride in an enclosed box on the LandCruiser 79 (UTE-GARAGE.md, report 17), which reuses this report's chocks, L-track, straps, winch and load cases. Where this report says the trailer's garage, read the ute's bike box.

## Summary

- Secure each bike with a removable Condor SC-2000 front wheel chock, four AS/NZS 4380 rated 35 mm ratchet straps on soft loops, and a fifth strap through the rear wheel. Anchor everything to two rows of flush-mount aluminium L-track that are through-bolted to steel rails under the floor.
- With the bikes across the trailer, trailer braking (0.8 g) pushes each bike sideways, so the high straps must stop it rolling over. That is the governing case. With an off-road factor of 2, the worst strap carries about 395 kgf (Tuareg, rear strap). Straps of LC 500 kg or more pass. I recommend LC 1,000 kg.
- Design every strap anchor and its floor fixing for 20 kN, because the NTC guide says anchors should withstand the lashing's breaking load.
- The Tuareg (2,220 mm) leaves only 110 mm of the 2,330 mm interior for the chock nose and wall padding. Check this first.
- Added weight is about 52 kg without the ramp and about 86 kg with the 34 kg full-width ramp. About 67 kg of that (chocks, straps, ramp) can come out when the bikes stay home. Added height is up to about 25 mm under the front wheel (an estimate, since the chock's cradle rise is not published) and under 1 mm under the rear wheel.

## 2. Load standard and why the layout matters

The NTC Load Restraint Guide for Light Vehicles 2018 ("Loading Performance Standards", page 19) requires a restraint that holds the load under each of these separately: 0.8 g deceleration forward, 0.5 g rearward, 0.5 g lateral, and 0.2 g vertical if friction or limited vertical movement is relied on. Your figures are correct ([NTC light vehicle guide, PDF](https://media.nrspp.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/06005924/Load-Restraint-Guide-for-light-vehicles-2018.pdf)). The guide covers vehicles and trailers up to 4.5 t GVM.

Rules from the full [NTC Load Restraint Guide 2018 (PDF, 65 MB)](https://www.transafewa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Load-Restraint-Guide-2018.pdf) used here:

- Direct lashing angle effect = tie-point distance in the restraint direction divided by lashing length. Required capacity per lashing = its share of the force divided by the angle effect (Cases 6 and 7, pages 221 to 226).
- Rubber-tyred vehicles: restrain forward and rearward directly, use tie-down friction only sideways, pre-tension to at least 20% of the load weight, and do not rely on brakes (pages 117 and 118).
- Lashings on one vehicle should be the same type, length and angle so they share load (page 106).
- To limit bounce, use vertical lashings at the wheels rated at least half the vehicle weight (page 120).
- Anchor points should withstand the minimum breaking load of the lashings fixed to them (page 201).

With the bikes across the trailer, braking acts sideways on each bike. An upright motorcycle has no gravity restoring moment in roll, so the high straps carry the whole overturning moment. In a fore-aft layout the 0.8 g case would push the tyre into the chock, which is much easier. I sized everything for 0.8 g in every horizontal direction, so it holds for either layout as long as the bikes face forward in a fore-aft layout.

## 3. Restraint calculation

### Off-road dynamic factor

I multiply the NTC horizontal cases by 2.0 and use 1.0 g upward instead of 0.2 g:

- The NTC standards describe road driving including emergency braking and minor collisions. They do not cover repeated impacts on corrugations and potholes.
- A load applied suddenly to an elastic restraint gives a peak force up to twice the static force (the standard result for a step load on an undamped spring). This models a strap that loses tension on corrugations and then snatches tight.
- The NTC guide treats bounce separately and asks for wheel lashings rated at half the vehicle weight (page 120).
- 1.0 g upward means the straps can hold the bike's full weight down while the floor drops away into a pothole.

I found no published measurement of camper-trailer body accelerations on Australian corrugations to calibrate this, so the engineer should accept or change it.

Method: overturning moment = m x g x 0.8 x CG height (Tuareg 1,132 N m, Triple 824 N m), divided between the leeward front and rear straps by their arms. Once the windward straps go slack, a leeward strap's tension equals its share of the moment over its arm, so moderate pre-tension does not add to the peak. The rearward case uses the front straps' forward direction cosines (Tuareg 0.49, Triple 0.53).

Sideways sliding at the base (330 kgf on the Tuareg with the factor) goes into the chock at the front, and into tyre friction plus the rear wheel strap at the rear. Strap tension raises the rear tyre load to about 3.5 kN, so friction at 0.5 gives about 180 kgf, enough for the rear half.

### Strap and anchor choice

- Need about 395 kgf per strap on the Tuareg and 301 kgf on the Triple.
- AS/NZS 4380 straps are rated by lashing capacity (LC), typically half the breaking load ([Juli Slings](https://julislings.com/australian-ratchet-tie-down-product/)). LC 500 kg gives a utilisation of 0.79 on the Tuareg, which passes. LC 1,000 kg gives 0.40. I recommend the 1,000 kg configuration; it weighs little more.
- A US 1.5 in strap at 1,000 lb WLL (454 kg) gives 0.87 utilisation, which passes but with thin margin.
- 25 mm straps of 250 kg LC (for example the SpanSet Weekender) fail.
- Anchor design load per NTC page 201: 9.8 kN for LC 500 kg straps, 13.3 kN for a 3,000 lb break US strap, 19.6 kN for LC 1,000 kg. Design every anchor and its floor fixing for 20 kN. The double-stud fitting (22.2 kN break) and Kinedyne's recessed pan (22.3 kN break) both meet that.

### Tensioning and fork protection

- Pull the front straps down until the forks compress about one third of their travel, and never bottom them ([Rhino USA](https://www.rhinousainc.com/blogs/news/how-to-tie-down-a-motorcycle)). That is about 80 mm on the Tuareg and 40 mm on the Triple. Snug the rear straps enough to take up a little rear sag.
- The compressed springs keep the straps taut when the trailer drops into a hole. A bottomed fork has no spring left, so impacts go straight into the straps and frame.
- At one third compression no fork brace is needed. "Fork saver" blocks fit between the tyre and a high front fender on dirt bikes and do not suit low-fender road bikes.
- Use ratchets, not cam buckles. Cam buckles are limited to hand tension, which suits light bikes but leaves little margin here.
- Re-check tension after the first 10 to 20 km and after any rough section (light vehicle guide, page 8).

## 10. Loading ramp

Ramp length L = H / sin A for floor height H and angle A.

| Floor height | 15 deg | 17 deg | 20 deg |
|---|---|---|---|
| 800 mm | 3,091 mm | 2,736 mm | 2,339 mm |
| 1,000 mm | 3,864 mm | 3,420 mm | 2,924 mm |

Aim for 15 degrees or less and never exceed 20 when walking a 206 kg bike. The only sources found are dealer guides, not standards: [Truck N Tow](https://www.truckntow.com/blogs/articles/loading-ramps-for-atv-and-motorcycle-trailers) recommends a 4:1 length-to-height ratio, which keeps the angle under about 14 degrees. Each 100 mm of floor height costs about 390 mm of ramp at 15 degrees.

The 3,048 mm Black Widow MF-12038 gives 15.2 degrees at 800 mm and 19.2 degrees at 1,000 mm. A 2,388 mm single runner (Black Widow BW-9417-HD, 19.5 kg per a retailer listing) gives 19.6 and 24.8 degrees, which is too steep for a 1,000 mm floor.

Breakover for the Street Triple (wheelbase 1,402 mm) on a straight ramp: the sill crest rises about 92 mm into the gap under the bike at 15.2 degrees and 115 mm at 19.2 degrees (wheelbase / 4 x sin A). The arched ramp reduces this. The Triple's ground clearance still needs measuring.

How it hooks on: the MF-12038's "attaching plates" rest on the edge of the loading surface, and its safety straps loop round the main rail 2 to 3 ft from the tip and tie to a secure point under the vehicle (instructions PDF). Fit an aluminium sill angle to the door threshold for the plates to sit on, and two tie points on the chassis below the door for the safety straps. Its 965 mm width lets the rider walk beside the bike on the ramp.

Door opening estimate for walking the Tuareg in nose first:

- Width about 1,250 mm: the 965 mm bar width, plus the rider walking on the left with the body about 300 mm beyond the bar end, plus about 50 mm clearance each side (estimate). This is also wider than the 965 mm ramp.
- Height about 1,450 mm clear: the 1,370 mm bike height, plus about 50 mm for the suspension extending off its sag, plus about 30 mm margin (estimate). The bike pitches nose-up on the ramp, which lowers its screen relative to the door head, so the level bike sets the limit.
- A bike cannot be shifted sideways once inside, so one door must line up with both straight-in paths (roughly 2.2 m wide, more than the garage length). Either fit one door per bay or accept a wheeled mover for one bike.

## 11. Garage safety with fuelled bikes under a bed

- Vents: petrol vapour is heavier than air. Fit at least two low vents at floor level on opposite sides and one high vent for heat. Camec (Australian) sells caravan vents and cowls; I did not select a product.
- Fume detector: Fireboy-Xintex gasoline fume detector. Alarm and red LED at 20% of the lower explosive limit, 9-18 or 18-30 V DC, -40 to 85 C, two sensors on 6 m extension cables, and a "BB" version with an 18 A blower relay that could run an extractor fan ([brochure PDF](https://productimageserver.com/literature/brochure/63875BR.pdf)). Mount the sensors low in the garage and the display where you sleep.
- Extinguisher: 2.5 kg ABE dry powder, 4A:40B:E (the B rating covers petrol), 4.3 kg gross, 130 x 383 mm, with vehicle bracket ([Firewize](https://shop.firewize.com.au/products/dry-chemical-powder-2-5kg-abe)). Mount it beside the garage door, outside the space you would be fighting a fire in. The lighter alternative is a FlameStop 2.0 kg ABE (3.6 kg, 3A:30B:E, 110 x 402 mm; [datasheet PDF](https://integratedsafetygroup.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/2KG-ABE-POWDER-FIRE-EXTINGUISHER.pdf), figures from the search listing because the PDF is an image).
- Optional automatic system: Fireboy-Xintex CG2 (automatic) or MA2 (automatic plus manual pull) clean-agent HFC-227ea unit. It discharges at 79 C (175 F) and floods the space to an 8.7% concentration. The garage holds about 6.8 m3 (2.0 x 2.33 x 1.45 m), so it needs the 250 ft3 (7.0 m3) size, MA20250227-BL or CG20250227-B: 160 mm diameter x 427 mm, 4.9 kg of agent, 14.0 kg shipping weight ([Fireboy manual PDF](https://www.fireboy.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/18014-MA2-CG2-HFC-227ea-REV-G.pdf)). It only works if the garage is sealed from the bed space. Not included in the totals.
- Add a heat alarm in the garage, interconnected with the smoke alarm in the living area. Product not chosen.

## 12. Alternatives considered and why they lost

- Chocks: see section 6. Steadystand Multi Fixed is the runner-up: 3.8 kg lighter per chock and distributed in Australia, but it bolts down, so the floor takes longer to clear.
- Anchors: see section 5. Recessed D-rings are the runner-up: strong and flush, but in fixed positions and needing deep cut-outs.
- Straps: 25 mm 250 kg LC straps fail. 50 mm straps are heavier and would push the anchor design load to about 40 kN. US WLL-rated 1.5 in straps pass with thin margin.
- Floor: tread plate adds 29 to 42 kg; coin mat about 23 kg and traps grit.
- Ramp: 2.4 m ramps are too steep for a 1,000 mm floor. Generic 340 kg imported ramps have no track record to judge.
